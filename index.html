Case study 1 of 3

People Analytics

A case study in why people leave, followed from a messy HR export to a decision-ready dashboard.

  • Psychology
  • Excel
  • SQL
  • Python
  • Power BI
  • Streamlit

Introduction

Welcome to my People Analytics case study. Employee turnover is expensive, and most organisations only see it clearly after it has happened. I wanted to work through the whole problem the way an analyst on an HR team would: start with a business question, receive a messy export, clean it, find the pattern and turn it into something a manager can act on.

I bring a psychology lens to the numbers. The survey scales in this project (satisfaction, involvement, work-life balance) are treated as measures of how people experience work, not just columns to average.

The company throughout is a fictional business called Meridian Works, and all of its data was generated for this project. To see the slide deck built for the analysis, click here.

The toolkit behind this project

Phase 1: Ask

What is the problem I am trying to solve?

Meridian Works, a fictional company of 1,500 employees, wants to understand its attrition rate and the factors driving it. With that knowledge, leadership can decide where to invest to keep people and lift overall job satisfaction. As the analyst, I provide insight into current attrition and the workforce survey.

  • The business task is to identify trends in employee attrition and interpret the employee survey data.
  • Which areas of the business are driving attrition?
  • At what point in their time with the company do people leave?
  • How did employees score the workplace on the survey, and where are the lowest scores?

A brief written from scratch

Nothing here comes from an off-the-shelf dataset. I defined the business problem first, then generated the data needed to investigate it.

Phase 2: Prepare & Process

Instead of downloading a static file from Kaggle, I used Python's Faker library to generate the data algorithmically. The result is a realistic, complex and deliberately uncleaned transactional employee sheet, matching the kind of messy file an HR department sends over: inconsistent spellings, mixed date formats, duplicate rows and missing values.

Cleaning a file like that is most of the real work in people analytics, so I made it part of the project. The dataset covers 1,500 employees across 3 departments and 11 job roles, with fields ranging from age and gender to pay, tenure and workplace survey scores.

What the HR export looks like

Cleaning in SQL

-- Keep the latest record for each employee
WITH latest AS (
  SELECT *,
         ROW_NUMBER() OVER (
           PARTITION BY employee_id
           ORDER BY exported_at DESC
         ) AS rn
  FROM raw_hr_export
)
SELECT
  employee_id,
  INITCAP(TRIM(full_name)) AS full_name,
  CASE
    WHEN LOWER(TRIM(department)) LIKE 'eng%' THEN 'Engineering'
    WHEN LOWER(TRIM(department)) LIKE 'sal%' THEN 'Sales'
    WHEN LOWER(TRIM(department)) IN ('hr', 'human resources') THEN 'Human Resources'
  END AS department,
  NULLIF(REGEXP_REPLACE(monthly_income, '[^0-9.]', '', 'g'), '')::numeric AS monthly_income,
  CASE WHEN job_satisfaction BETWEEN 1 AND 4 THEN job_satisfaction END AS job_satisfaction
FROM latest
WHERE rn = 1;

An illustrative excerpt in PostgreSQL syntax.

  • Duplicates. Repeated exports were collapsed so each employee has one row.
  • Text. Labels such as "Sal3s", "Eng." and "HR" were mapped to three clean department names.
  • Dates. Five date formats were parsed into ISO dates, with day-first values checked against the export's regional format.
  • Numbers. Currency symbols and separators were stripped, and impossible incomes were set to null.
  • Survey answers. Text answers such as "high" were recoded to the 1 to 4 scale, and out-of-range scores were nulled.
  • Structure. The flat sheet was split into employees, employment history, departments, job roles and survey responses.

Phase 3: Analyze

I began by calculating attrition for the whole workforce and then for each department. Meridian Works has 1,500 employees and an attrition rate of 17.4%. As I added detail on active versus terminated employment, it became clear which departments and job roles were running above the company rate.

After a general overview by department and job role, I looked for patterns in age and years at company, then in the survey scores. The full set of results is in the slide deck and Power BI dashboards further down.

What stood out

  • Sales and Human Resources lose people fastest, at 21.5% and 21.7% against a company rate of 17.4%. Engineering sits at 15.1%.
  • Early tenure is the danger zone: 69% of all departures happen within the first three years.
  • Three roles stand out: Sales Development Rep (32.0%), QA Analyst (23.2%) and HR Partner (22.9%).
  • Those same roles score among the lowest on the survey. Sales Development Reps rate job satisfaction 2.50 out of 4, and QA Analysts rate their environment 2.52.

Phase 5: Act

Recommendations

  • Collect better exit data. Record the termination type, the effective date and exit interview themes, so the reasons behind high-attrition areas can be understood and not just counted.
  • Build an early-tenure retention programme. With most departures in the first three years, focus onboarding, mentoring and check-ins on Sales Development Reps, QA Analysts and HR Partners.
  • Develop departmental retention initiatives that strengthen working relationships and satisfaction, with a goal of growing average company seniority.
  • Run quarterly surveys to monitor how employee sentiment changes after each initiative.

The pattern behind all four recommendations is a psychology one, not just an operations one: people's decisions to stay or go are shaped by specific experiences at specific moments, not by an abstract average. The next case study tests that same idea on customers instead of employees.

Customer Analytics

Why subscribers actually cancel, and whether one bad moment matters more than a season of mediocre ones.

  • Psychology
  • Python
  • SQL
  • Power BI
  • Streamlit
  • Behavioral Economics

The Behavioral Hook

A hypothesis, not just a dashboard

Solmere is a fictional direct-to-consumer subscription business built for this project: 8,400 active subscribers, a monthly home and wellness box, all data synthetic. Most churn dashboards average a subscriber's satisfaction over their whole lifetime and compare that average between people who stay and people who leave. That treats every touchpoint as equally memorable, which is not how people actually judge an experience.

Daniel Kahneman's peak-end rule suggests otherwise: people judge an experience mainly by its most intense point and by how it ended, not by the average of every moment along the way. Applied to a subscription, the hypothesis is that a single sharp negative moment shortly before a renewal decision predicts cancellation far better than a subscriber's overall satisfaction score does.

The question I'm testing

Does a negative peak moment close to the renewal date predict cancellation better than tenure or average satisfaction alone?

  • Do subscribers who file a support ticket in the fourteen days before renewal cancel at a higher rate than their satisfaction history would predict?
  • Is a single bad delivery more predictive of cancellation than a string of ordinary ones?
  • Does the quality of the first thirty days, the onboarding peak, predict twelve-month churn better than raw tenure does?

The Data Pipeline

As with the people analytics project, nothing here is downloaded from an existing source. Python's Faker library generated two years of subscriber, order and support-ticket records for Solmere, with the same kind of mess a real export carries: duplicate orders, inconsistent cancellation-reason free text, mixed currency formatting and missing sentiment scores on older tickets.

SQL then rebuilt the flat export into relational tables and engineered the fields the hypothesis actually needs: days between a subscriber's most recent negative support ticket and their next renewal date, and a simple onboarding-quality score from their first thirty days.

The pipeline

  1. Step 1

    Python and Faker

    Synthesise subscriber, order and support-ticket history, with realistic errors left in.

  2. Step 2

    SQL

    Clean the export and derive the moment-timing fields the hypothesis needs.

  3. Step 3

    Power BI

    Model the tables and visualise churn against moment quality.

Deriving the moment-timing field

-- Flag subscribers with a negative support event
-- shortly before their renewal decision
SELECT
  s.subscriber_id,
  s.renewal_date,
  MAX(t.opened_at) FILTER (
    WHERE t.sentiment = 'negative'
  ) AS last_negative_ticket,
  s.renewal_date - MAX(t.opened_at) FILTER (
    WHERE t.sentiment = 'negative'
  ) AS days_before_renewal,
  s.cancelled
FROM subscribers s
LEFT JOIN support_tickets t
  ON t.subscriber_id = s.subscriber_id
 AND t.opened_at BETWEEN s.renewal_date - INTERVAL '90 days'
                      AND s.renewal_date
GROUP BY s.subscriber_id, s.renewal_date, s.cancelled;

An illustrative excerpt in PostgreSQL syntax.

  • Duplicates. Retried checkouts had created repeat order rows, collapsed to one per transaction.
  • Free text. Cancellation reasons typed by support agents were mapped to a fixed set of categories.
  • Currency. Order values in mixed symbols and separators were normalised to a single numeric column.
  • Sentiment. Around 2,300 support tickets were scored; tickets from before sentiment scoring existed were left null rather than guessed at.
  • Structure. The flat sheet was split into subscribers, orders, deliveries and support tickets.

Strategic Interventions

The recommendations below follow directly from the peak-end hypothesis, not from the churn number on its own.

  • Close negative peaks fast. Route any support ticket flagged negative to a recovery follow-up within 48 hours, so the moment doesn't sit unresolved into the renewal window.
  • Engineer a positive peak before renewal. Time appreciation or loyalty messaging to land shortly before the decision point, using the same peak-end logic in reverse.
  • Treat the first 30 days as the priority, not tenure. Onboarding quality predicts churn more strongly than how long someone has subscribed, so retention budget should follow it there.
  • Track moment quality alongside average satisfaction. The two measures tell different stories, and only one of them lines up with who actually leaves.

Digital Product UX

An onboarding form was losing more than half its signups. The question was whether that was a motivation problem or a friction problem.

  • Psychology
  • Python
  • SQL
  • A/B Testing
  • Data Visualization

The Behavioral Hook

Motivation, ability, or trigger

Tallywell is a fictional personal-finance app built for this project. Its twelve-field signup form was converting well below the team's target, and the usual instinct was to fix it with better copy: clearer benefit statements, more reassuring language, a stronger call to action. That's a motivation fix, aimed at a problem that might not be about motivation at all.

BJ Fogg's behavior model holds that a behavior happens when motivation, ability and a trigger converge at the same moment; if any one of the three is too low, the behavior doesn't occur, however strong the other two are. Applied here, the hypothesis is that the drop-off is an ability problem: the form itself is the barrier, not the person's willingness to fill it in.

The question I'm testing

Will cutting friction from the form lift conversion more than a parallel motivational-copy change does?

  • Is drop-off concentrated at specific fields, an ability signal, or spread evenly through the form, which would point to motivation instead?
  • Does shortening the form lift completion more than adding trust and benefit copy around it?
  • Do people who abandon the form return later without a reminder, suggesting motivation was intact and ability was the blocker?

The Data Pipeline

Python's Faker library generated roughly 40,000 synthetic onboarding sessions, split across two variants: Variant A, the existing twelve-field form, and Variant B, a four-field form with the remaining details collected later through progressive profiling once someone is already active. Device, browser and time-of-day noise were added so the funnel behaves like a real one.

SQL rebuilt the raw event log into a step-by-step funnel per variant and computed per-field abandonment for Variant A, which is where the ability-versus-motivation question actually gets answered.

The pipeline

  1. Step 1

    Python and Faker

    Synthesise 40,000 onboarding sessions across two form variants.

  2. Step 2

    SQL

    Build the funnel by step and variant, and flag where each session dropped.

  3. Step 3

    Power BI

    Visualise the funnel and the conversion gap between variants.

Building the funnel

-- Conversion rate by variant and funnel step
SELECT
  variant,
  step_name,
  COUNT(*) AS sessions_reached,
  COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE completed_step)
    AS sessions_completed,
  ROUND(
    COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE completed_step)::numeric
    / COUNT(*), 3
  ) AS step_conversion
FROM onboarding_events
GROUP BY variant, step_name, step_order
ORDER BY variant, step_order;

An illustrative excerpt in PostgreSQL syntax.

  • Assignment checks. Sessions with a broken or missing variant flag were dropped rather than guessed into a bucket.
  • Bot traffic. Sessions completing the full form in under two seconds were excluded as non-human.
  • Timestamps. Event times from three client time zones were normalised to UTC before ordering the funnel.
  • Field-level drop-off. The exact field a session stalled on was parsed out of the raw event payload for Variant A only.

Strategic Interventions

Three fields accounted for well over half of all abandonment. That's a friction signature, not a motivation one, so the recommendations follow the ability side of the model.

  • Ship the short form as the default. Variant B's ability-side fix outperformed a parallel motivational-copy test that ran alongside it during the same window.
  • Move deferred fields to progressive profiling. Ask for the details cut from signup once someone is active and has already found value, when motivation is naturally higher.
  • Audit the next-highest drop step. The same field-level abandonment analysis should be run on the step after onboarding, where a smaller but similar drop remains.
  • Re-run tests as a habit, not a one-off. The thread through all three case studies is the same: behavior follows specific moments and specific frictions, not the average of how people say they feel. That's worth testing again next quarter, not just once.

Case study · Solmere ecosystem

Strategic Organizational Transformation

A post-acquisition culture and engagement recovery inside Solmere, the same fictional company from the Customer Analytics case study, seen this time through an organizational psychology lens rather than a data one: same business, a different kind of diagnosis.

Problem statement

Following its acquisition by a larger holding group, Solmere merged three previously independent teams, support, fulfilment and product, into a single reporting structure inside six weeks. The transition produced immediate operational friction: unclear ownership across the new department lines, a measurable drop in cross-team psychological safety, and engagement scores sliding across every monitored team.

Diagnostic framework

Edgar Schein's organizational culture model separated what each team said it valued from what actually got rewarded day to day, surfacing where the merged teams' norms genuinely conflicted rather than just clashed in style. The Job Demands-Resources model then mapped where rising demands, ambiguous reporting lines, duplicated approvals, were outpacing the resources, autonomy, peer support, recognition, available to absorb them.

Intervention strategy

A structured Change Management Playbook targeted the specific gaps the diagnostic surfaced, not generic culture-building:

  • Structured feedback loops — a fixed cadence of cross-team retrospectives, replacing ad hoc escalation.
  • Cross-functional alignment spaces — a shared decision log and a weekly sync for the three merged teams specifically.
  • Peer-led recognition protocols — recognition moved from manager-only to peer-nominated, addressing the resource side of the JD-R gap directly.

Strategic outcomes

+28%

Workplace involvement index, monitored teams

Within two quarters of rollout, workplace involvement indices recovered 28% across the monitored teams and the cross-team escalation backlog, the loudest complaint in exit interviews, was cleared. The playbook itself was documented as a repeatable framework, since reused for onboarding Solmere's next distributed hires.
